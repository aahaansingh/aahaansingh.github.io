My family keeps many things in the basement.
This was an oversight in my youth—it once got flooded with shit thanks to a pipe getting clogged by a sandwich that my sister flushed down the toilet.

Thankfully, it is an extensive basement, so the most important relic that it houses—our 400-strong library of Amar Chitra Kathas—was safe from damage.
ACKs are these little

comic books that cover tales from India’s vast corpus of history, mythology, and folklore.
Though ubiquitous in India, they are not very easy to get here.
I bought one as a gift for my friend Aadi at Alley Cat Comics (kudos to them for stocking it at all) and it was eight bucks, even though I’m sure they sell for less than one in the homeland.
They’re very typical of Indian comic books, which tend to have a blotchy Silver-Age style and moralistic narratives, but are still a lot more enjoyable than any of the older American comics that I’ve

As such, they are one of the main reasons that I know anything about where I came from.

I was especially interested in their history comics—they cover most eras of the history of the subcontinent, but I was particularly obsessed with their stories about great medieval and early modern rulers, like Akbar, Shivaji, Prithviraj Chauhan, and Sher Shah Suri.
These personages all faced catastrophic conflicts, from which they either emerged victorious or perished, and such high stakes are compelling to a child.
It is, however, difficult to comprehend the scale and dimensionality of such conflict, and ACKs generally take the route of flattening them into good-versus-evil narratives.
It was getting into grand strategy games, of all things, that made me realize that there are other ways of viewing history.

The “grand strategy” genre of game sees the individual taking control of a state in some respect (politically, militarily, economically) over a continuous period of time, with the objective of bringing about its success.
Paradox’s Europa Universalis 4 is the quintessential grand strategy game, allowing the player to play as a nation from the Fall of Constantinople in 1453 through the 19th Century.
This is primarily a function of conducting warfare, since the game’s scoring system heavily biases towards expansion, but more broadly includes economic and administrative aspects of realm management.
EU4 does a good job of modeling the capabilities of the state, in terms of its ability to wage war, deal with its subjects, and engage in diplomacy with other states.
This makes for a very rich game, because though the player may act in an ahistorical manner, they are bound by historical rules and thus can imagine their in-game accomplishments to be historical.

Temporally, EU4 sits directly after Crusader Kings 2, also made by Paradox, which covers the world over the course of the Middle Ages.
By rights, CK2 should not be a particularly good historical game because it doesn’t effectively model the capabilities of a state.
Diplomacy is ad-hoc, maintaining the stability of large realms is trivial, and warfare mostly consists of having the most troops.
Even on hard mode, the AI is pretty dumb, so taking over the entire map is easy after a point.
It is not a game of international statecraft by any means, and it does not aspire to be.
Unlike EU4, the player controls an individual ruler and—though explicitly tasked with increasing the “prestige” of their dynasty—has the flexibility to pursue whatever ends they desire.
This includes the ridiculous—there is a Steam achievement for conquering the entirety of the Indian subcontinent as a Norse ruler, and another for producing a multitude of inbred offspring.
I myself once conquered most of Asia as the King of Georgia, which, for various reasons that the game does not see fit to model, is borderline nonsensical.
The point is, though, that both the era and the lax mechanics of the game allow you to do weird things, and to do them as an individual.

Without the straitjacket of having to act as a state within a precise historical environment, CK2 is a different kind of fun from EU4.
You realize pretty quickly that the point of CK2 isn’t world conquest precisely because of how easy it is to do so.
There definitely exist speedrunners who play to blob but that just seems to me like self-punishment on multiple levels.
It’s like seeing who can eat a brick the fastest.
The most fun runs, to me, are those that have fascinating stories with a degree of plausibility, because it’s more interesting to shape the story of an individual than the land area of a country.
Recently, I played a game as the Count of Chalons in 936, in which
{{< sidenote "I" >}}While the character's stats restrict the range of choices available, decisions rest with the player, which is why I would consider myself to be performing these actions rather than my character. It would be a pretty stupid "game" otherwise.{{< /sidenote >}}
spent the first forty years trying to prop up an incompetent Burgundian Kingdom against the greater powers of France and the Holy Roman Empire. I could have taken over the kingdom myself but it would have been both a far less interesting premise and a sure way to destabilize the realm.
Instead, I was able to go the route of personal diplomacy: bribing away enemies, funding proxy wars, encouraging foreign rebels, contributing troops, and killing other rulers.
In most grand strategy games, such low-level maneuvering gets hand-waved away because the state is fundamentally envisioned as a unified whole, and internal dissent is merely an abstract obstacle to be overcome.
This is often an informed choice, because many modern theories of international relations do envision states as acting rationally and coherently, but those theories do not map so easily to pre-modern polities, which were less organized and more fragmented in governance.
Crusader Kings is therefore a game of individuals and how they interact—and at the individual level, different forces hold
{{< sidenote "sway." >}}Historian Bret Devereaux has a wonderful series on his blog about the historical positions that EU4 and CK3 take, and it goes into far more depth than I do over here.{{< /sidenote >}}

The most obvious of the new considerations is intra-realm politics.
In EU4, the integrity of the realm is rarely a pressing concern unless you are expanding rapidly—and even then, external coalitions present a more pressing threat.
Again, this boils down to the game’s vision of a state as being unified by default.
Crusader Kings is set in a period where centralized governance had long since broken down and would not emerge until the early-modern period.
Instead, rulers granted land for followers to administer in exchange for their loyalty, only for them to grant land to _their_ followers to administer in exchange for _their_ loyalty, and so on.
Over time, the privileges of these nobles became entrenched, so rulers would have to contend with individuals who, while nominally subordinates, often eclipsed them in power.
This struggle is clearly reflected in Crusader Kings, which takes the same tree-like approach to modelling land ownership.
Working together, vassals can swiftly neuter the realm or topple a ruler, so the highest priority for a player in most settings is to dominate their vassals, whether by winning allies or defeating rebels.
Once this is done, expansion is usually not so difficult.

An interesting consequence of Crusader Kings focusing more on the nitty-gritty of government is that it exposes you to the unsavoury aspects of managing a realm.
Morality holds no weight in EU4 because trying to make purely moral decisions will probably result in your state collapsing within fifty years.
It is still possible to run a state in an ethical manner (we can only hope so) but doing so is inconvenient in the game and besides the point.
More insidiously, the moral consequences of the state’s decisions are abstracted away by the game itself: when you convert the culture of a province in game, what you see is an increase in administrative control, and what you don’t see is what Israel is
{{< sidenote "doing" >}}Another point that Devereaux eloquently makes. He argues, seeing the number of people jumping from EU4 into history, that teachers should really emphasize the human cost of these decisions.{{< /sidenote >}}
in Gaza.
A large portion of the map is “uncolonized land,” as though its natural state were to be colonized.
CK2, on the other hand, makes you think about such inconveniences of governance, since you actually have to put some effort into murdering opponents.
To carry out a political assassination, you need to bribe the appropriate people, recruit them to your scheme, and wait in suspense in the hope that the decision doesn’t backfire and get you implicated in the process.
Killing is done with intent.
People still do it, because it’s still frequently convenient and occasionally funny, but it is a decision that is actively and informedly taken.
Even war touches the character, as rulers often face injury or death in battle.
When choices are personal, so are the consequences.

More than merely allowing for some level of ahistoric agency, however, the game frequently embraces absurdity.
There are events in which comets bestow magical bonuses, buildings that confer everlasting youth, and possessed children who will murder your bloodline.
If you play your cards right, you can get an immortal horse elected as the next pope.
Clearly, none of this is remotely historical, because we know that such things are impossible.
However, we live in a world of far more certainty than anybody in Western Europe did during the Middle Ages.
People used to believe in dragons and witchcraft.
Part of the mission that the pope assigned to John of Plano Carpini, in his trip to the Mongol capital, was to seek out
{{< sidenote "Prester John," >}}Amusingly, many western clergy at the time ascribed the person of Prester John to various historical figures, among them the Khitan conqueror Yelü Dashi (who was actually Buddhist).{{< /sidenote >}}
a mythical Christian ruler in the east who was said to be lord over seventy-two kings and to possess unimaginable riches.
The fact that he came upon a kingdom of unknown tribesmen who had already subjugated a land of unimaginable riches and would conquer their way to Poland is no less fantastical.
Some of the truths that we take for granted today are surely stranger than the fictions that people took for granted in the society of Crusader Kings.
As such, a modicum of imagination is required to situate oneself in the magical uncertainty of the past.

It is unsurprising, then, that the Crusader Kings titles have cultivated such a unique and diverse community.
The Paradox forums play host to an immense number of playthroughs and writeups, which are essentially historical fanfiction.
I never really participated because writing a compelling story is hard.
Plus, most writeups are pretty light on historicity for the same reason that the game is fun for roleplaying.
Trying to make my games perfectly plausible is itself not a plausible endeavor with how simplified the game mechanics are.
In particular, the rigid way in which it characterizes “feudalism” is conducive to gameplay but hardly

However, Crusader Kings is already considered to be an immensely complex game that is difficult to get into.
Trying to make it realistic would probably turn many people off for the benefit of the few people out there who play The Campaign for North Africa, and it would get rid of a lot of the fun bits.
There’s no good solution to the realism problem for a mass-market game, though EU5 (Paradox’s latest title) takes a step towards flattening a long learning curve by upping the amount of detail available to the player while enabling them to automate the parts of gameplay that they can’t handle.
For the most part, though, if I want a realistic hi-story, I might as well crack open an actual history book.

Indeed, that is where a lot of the utility of the game lies.
It is difficult to roleplay if you do not have any context on the character you’re playing, so you’d better read up on them first, and maybe on the history of their kingdom while you’re at it, and oh, you might as well learn about the society in which they lived.
Playing an alternate history often makes me wonder whether real events were really that crazy—and they often were!
I also think that, like most games, it becomes hacky when you get good—in that you learn to use mechanics in ways that were not intended—so after a certain point, I got motivated to learn about the time period from outside the game’s perspective.
Crusader Kings was a major factor in getting me into History Bowl in {{< backlink "note12" "high school" >}}, and it encouraged my interest in the Mongols and European geopolitics.
The map is also a great way to learn Eurasian geography, because it’s three-dimensional, gives names in the native language when possible, and pinpoints who ruled what and when to an astonishing degree of precision.
It is also a thousand years out of date but many major settlements have persisted for a long time.

After a couple thousand hours of Crusader Kings, I have been left with a lot of useless knowledge about Medieval Europe.
I could justify it by saying that learning about the past helps us understand the present, and that is true—you could identify a causal chain of events between the actions of Charlemagne and WWII.
Being able to identify that chain is also not an incredibly useful skill in most contexts.
Really, I just find the knowledge itself to be interesting, and playing the game to be fun.
That’s the whole point.
