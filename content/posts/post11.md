The Mahabharata is as contorted and branched as a narrative has ever naturally become.
Though the poem balances upon the events of the Battle of Kurukshetra, it twists through the tales of generations before and after, the distant causes and enduring consequences of this internecine struggle.

So, too, does it reach through multiple frames of narration to discuss events of only tangential importance to the core story—as with the case of Vyasa (at a yajna) reciting the recitation of a mongoose (at a yajna) of a story wherein the mongoose half turned to gold after visiting the house of a righteous Brahmin.
As with most epics, this is a consequence of the Mahabharata undergoing myriad additions and subtractions at the hands of various interpreters over hundreds of years before being written down.

It is tempting, then, to dismiss the
{{< sidenote "ending" >}}Well, in the same way that the Greek epic cycle "ends" with Telegonus killing Odysseus.{{< /sidenote >}}
of the Mahabharata, narrated in the Svargarohana Parva, as a later addition to the story.
In this final book, Yudhisthira enters heaven alive to see Duryodhana residing prosperously amongst the gods.
Angered, he asks to see his brothers and his wife, and he finds them anguished in hell.
Driven to grief, Yudhisthira rejects dharma and elects to stay with his family in hell rather than enjoy the fruits of his righteous life in heaven.
The gods appear before Yudhisthira to tell him that he was merely viewing an _illusion_ of hell as a last test of his character (for he had been tested before on his path to heaven), and he finally ascends.
This is a jarring conclusion, and two elements of the final end stick out to me as a reader—first, that Duryodhana is accepted to heaven, and second, that Yudhisthira, the _dharmaraja_, comes to censure dharma itself.
Can these be reconciled with the rest of the text?

Duryodhana, the chief antagonist of the Mahabharata (if one exists), is killed in perhaps the poem's climactic scene.
Having retreated under a lake upon the destruction of nearly his entire army, he wallows in
{{< sidenote "self-pity" >}}A motif throughout the poem—recall that the Bhagavad Gita begins with such an episode on Arjuna's part. Both fantasize about abandoning their duty as warriors and are compelled to change their minds.{{< /sidenote >}}
and refuses to emerge until he is goaded into a prideful rage and promised single combat for control over the kingdom.
Being among the most accomplished warriors with the mace, he challenges Bhima to a duel with his favored weapon.
In the ensuing battle, Bhima realizes through Krishna's subtle cues that he can only win through deceit and strikes Duryodhana down on his thigh (thus consummating an earlier promise of revenge from the dice game) to fell him.
As Duryodhana lays dying, celestials rain praise and offerings upon him, seemingly recognizing his death to have been honorable.
Thus does he ascend to heaven, where Yudhisthira meets him at the very end.

Duryodhana is not what we would consider a _good_ person by any stretch of the imagination.
He is also not what the text would consider to be a good person, which can be inferred very easily by the fact that his birth is accompanied by a series of comically bad
{{< sidenote "omens" >}}"As soon as Duryodhana was born, he began to cry and bray like an ass. And hearing that sound, the asses, vultures, jackals and crows uttered their respective cries responsively. Violent winds began to blow, and there were fires in various directions."; I'm using the Ganguli translation as it's the only complete one I could get for free.{{< /sidenote >}}
and is subsequently prophesied to be the "exterminator of the [Kuru] race" (1.115).
It would be difficult to provide a complete list of his horrible misdeeds, so I will name the ones that most immediately come to mind.
First, he attempts to assassinate the Pandavas by arranging for their stay in a house made of highly flammable lacquer, to be set aflame in the dead of night; it is only the assistance of Vidura that spares the brothers.
Later, grievously jealous of the power and popularity of the Pandavas, he challenges Yudhisthira to a game of dice, upon which Yudhisthira stakes all of his possessions—including his kingdom—and loses, forcing him and his family into thirteen years of exile.
This episode contains the chief grievance of the Pandavas, the attempted disrobing and humiliation of their wife, Draupadi, when she is lost in the game.
Lastly, upon the fulfilment of the conditions of the exile, Duryodhana still refuses the Pandavas their share of the kingdom, leading to the disastrous civil war and the near-extermination of the Kuru race.
Given the fact that the Mahabharata is centered around the question of who _ought_ to possess the kingdom, it is curious that the loser in both the moral and worldly domains is rewarded upon death.

What, then, does the final chapter suggest?
One possible meaning is that the ending negates the rest of the story.
The war kills generations of warriors and causes immense worldly suffering, but the ending may suggest that these consequences are
{{< sidenote "material," >}}I cannot claim an understanding of the philosophy of the Mahabharata or Gita sufficient to properly untangle this particular aspect of the text, but the illusion of the material world is a significant theme.{{< /sidenote >}}
and thus, immaterial.
After all, Yudhisthira is "divested of all his enmities and grief" (18.3) before finally entering heaven.
He is thus stripped of all that attaches him to the physical world, where the tale takes place.

Perhaps this is part of the reason, but it would make for a truly terrible anticlimax.
The Mahabharata is supposed to be entertaining as well as didactic, and given that this negation being the sole justification for the ending would make me extremely irritated, I have trouble believing it to be so.
Furthermore, if worldly actions did not matter, then the story would be in contradiction (as opposed to merely being unimportant) because it is centered upon questions of how one ought to act.
If the ending is integral to the main story, as appears to be the modern consensus[^fn:1], then this cannot be the case.

A slightly different interpretation is more functional: the sight of Duryodhana in heaven sets up Yudhisthira's final test, in which he elects to stay with his brothers in hell rather than enjoy the fruits of his actions and is thus confirmed to be righteous.
In making this decision, Yudhisthira states the following:
> What is that act by doing which Dhritarashtra’s son, king Suyodhana, with all his sinful followers, has become invested with such prosperity? Endued with prosperity like that of the great Indra himself, he is highly adored. What is that act through the consequence of which these (high-souled ones) have fallen into Hell? (18.3)

He is therefore arguing that there is no standard by which Duryodhana (named under the epithet Suyodhana) could achieve heaven while his brothers were consigned to hell, and that the consequences meted out to his brothers are thus unjust.
However, I am not sure that Duryodhana's fate is necessary to this argument.
Yudhisthira goes on to assert:
> All of them were conversant with every duty, were heroes, were devoted to truth and the Vedas; were observant of Kshatriya practices; were righteous in their acts; were performers of sacrifices; and givers of large presents unto brahmanas.

Clearly, Yudhisthira understands what the duty of his brothers was on earth—and, his hell being an illusion, he is vindicated—so he has no need to look at Duryodhana's actions for guidance as to the nature of this duty.
In other words, Yudhisthira knew that his brothers were undeserving of hell, regardless of Duryodhana's presence in heaven, and would thus have chosen to stay with them anyways.
So while Duryodhana lends some drama to the situation, this is not necessarily a good reason to place him in heaven.

Other potential reasons present themselves in the aftermath of Yudhisthira's decision.
His choice to remain in hell causes the gods to appear, and Yama (his father, the god of death and dharma) tells him that he has passed the final test, explaining its necessity:
> Hell, O son, should without doubt be beheld by every king. Of both good and bad there is abundance, O chief of men. He who enjoys first the fruits of his good acts must afterwards endure Hell. He, on the other hand, who first endures Hell, must afterwards enjoy Heaven. He whose sinful acts are many enjoys Heaven first. It is for this, O king, that desirous of doing thee good, I caused thee to be sent for having a view of Hell. Thou hadst, by a pretence, deceived Drona in the matter of his son. Thou hast, in consequence thereof, been shown Hell by an act of deception. (18.4)

Yama mentions the most well known reason that Yudhisthira is made to see hell, which is that Yudhisthira tricked Drona into believing that his Drona's son was
{{< sidenote "dead." >}}The only time that Dharmaraja ever employed deception, though it was not technically a lie.{{< /sidenote >}}
The illusion of hell thus counterbalances the illusion of truth.
However, a few other reasons are mentioned in the above dialogue.
One is that Yudhisthira is a king and that all kings must see hell.
"Must" implies that every king is required to see hell, but it seems more likely that the need for all kings to see hell is a consequence of kings accumulating sin over the course of their rule.
Bhishma argues in the Santi Parva that rulers must be willing to mete out violence to preserve order (12.69), but if violence is a moral stain, then there is no way for the ruler to escape punishment.
The other reason is that Yudhisthira did good things on earth, was rewarded for them in life (for he won the kingdom), and thus had to endure hell afterwards; Duryodhana, whose sins were many, enjoys heaven immediately.
Duryodhana suffered much in life, but I am not sure whether that is reason enough for him to skip hell, especially since the majority of Yudhisthira's family was also killed in the war.
The statement could mean that Duryodhana will experience hell at some point in the future but that his sinfulness delays his descent, but that seems to me even more ridiculous.
Why would sinfulness have any bearing on the order of punishment?
The statement might not be contrasting Yudhisthira with Duryodhana at all, because Duryodhana does display virtue in friendship, war, and death, but Duryodhana should then still have entered hell at _some_ point.

Let us return to the functional argument, because there are two events that make the situation more subtle than an initial impression might indicate.
First, while Yudhisthira initially enters heaven without having died (that is, his physical body intact), he is purified and achieves his celestial form after the final test.
This creates a distinction between his first and second periods in heaven, because he is still tied to material reality in the first.
Second, while the Mahabharata takes great pains to list where everybody ends up after death, Duryodhana's fate is not mentioned.
It is possible that the poem is merely avoiding redundancy, but the prop-like way in which Duryodhana is treated over the course of the final book seems like an indication that his very presence in heaven is part of the illusion.
Duryodhana's presence does not determine Yudhisthira's later choice, but it does deepen his anguish; in other words, he is very much a part of the test.
In this reading, Duryodhana may not have ascended in the first place, which preserves the moral continuity of the poem while enhancing the narrative and emotional tension of the last test.

I favor this interpretation, but it is a tenuous one.
Yama never states that Duryodhana's presence in heaven is part of the test, and it is difficult to reconcile with the praise heaped upon Duryodhana in the setting of his death.
It also does not explain Yama's curious statement about the sinful enjoying heaven immediately.
All we know for sure is that this ending is not a very coherent one.

In any case, Yudhisthira's rationale for rejecting dharma is now clear: he perceives, in his illusion, a universe that does not operate according to dharma, and, in that moment, believes dharma to be meaningless.
His identity is defined by adherence to dharma to such an extent that it is difficult to separate Yudhisthira as a character from Yudhisthira as an embodiment of righteousness.
It is his pivotal lie and his rejection of dharma that actually make him sympathetic as an individual.
The scene is incredibly impactful as a result, the culmination of the king's years of guilt and trauma, as well as the last judgement of his character.
Interestingly, it is also with precedent in the poem, as Karna also censures dharma for abandoning him as his chariot sinks into the ground:
> Unable to endure all those calamities, he waved his arms and began to rail at righteousness saying, "They that are conversant with righteousness always say that righteousness protects those that are righteous. As regards ourselves, we always endeavour, to the best of our ability and knowledge to practise righteousness. That righteousness, however, is destroying us now instead of protecting us that are devoted to it. I, therefore, think that righteousness does not always protect its worshippers." (8.90)

Notably, neither episode really threatens the integrity of dharma as a concept.
Yudhisthira is only angry at a mirage, while Karna reaps the consequences of killing a Brahmin's cow, for which he is cursed to this exact fate.
Instead, these utterances function more as ultimate expressions of anguish—again, both characters live and die by their moral codes, and to renounce them reinforces the desperation of their situations.
I also think that they serve to acknowledge the complexity of dharma and karma, because Karna's curses really are unjust, and Yudhisthira's situation is so tangled and knotted that we still don't really know what it's supposed to mean.

It would be nice if the Mahabharata resolved cleanly, but it's not really possible to satisfactorily close a story whose depth and breadth stretch almost beyond comprehension.
There are too many questions asked, too many separate threads, and too many authors with too many opinions for any sort of coherent purpose to emerge at the very end.
That is precisely what makes the Mahabharata such a compelling work: in its universe of subtlety and illusion, the bad ending is a good one.
